That is the short version, and it runs against a common instinct. We often think the most spiritual way to disciple someone is to keep it open: "What do you feel God is saying to you?" With a mature believer, that can be a good question. With a brand-new one, it usually just hands them back their own untrained impressions and calls it God.

## New Believers Cannot Yet Hear God Well

The Bible is honest that hearing God and discerning his will is something you grow into, not something you have the day you believe. New Christians are spiritual infants, and Scripture says so plainly:

<ScriptureBlock reference="Hebrews 5:13-14">
Anyone who lives on milk, being still an infant, is not acquainted with the teaching about righteousness. But solid food is for the mature, who by constant use have trained themselves to distinguish good from evil.
</ScriptureBlock>

Read that last line slowly. The ability to distinguish, to tell what is God and what is not, comes "by constant use." It is trained, over time, through practice. A new believer has not done the reps yet. So asking them to navigate life by their own sense of God's voice is like handing a newborn a steak. It is not more mature. It sets them up to mistake their own feelings, or worse, for God.

## So You Start High-Directive

This is why early discipleship is deliberately high-directive. You do not leave a new believer to figure it out. You give them clear, plain things to obey: the commands of Jesus, a simple next step, a tool to practice. You tell them, kindly and directly, here is what following Jesus looks like this week, let's do it.

High-directive does not mean controlling or harsh. It means clear. It is the kindness of not pretending a beginner already knows what they do not. Every good teacher is directive with a beginner and gives them room as they grow. It is exactly the high-directive move behind the [starter tools](/discipleship-training-curriculum/) and the [commands of Christ](/commands-of-christ-training/): give a new disciple something concrete and clear to obey, not a blank page.

<PullQuote>Asking a brand-new believer what they sense God saying usually just hands them back their own voice and calls it his.</PullQuote>

## Obedience Is How They Learn to Hear God

Here is the part that makes the whole paradigm work, and it is good news. Obeying what Jesus clearly said is not a lesser, beginner substitute for hearing God. It is the very path to hearing him. Jesus tied the two together:

<ScriptureBlock reference="John 14:21">
Whoever has my commands and keeps them is the one who loves me. The one who loves me will be loved by my Father, and I too will love them and show myself to them.
</ScriptureBlock>

Keep his commands, and he shows himself to you. Obedience to the clear comes first, and deeper knowing follows. As a new believer obeys the plain things Jesus said, forgive, pray, tell the truth, love this person, share your story, they start to recognize his voice and his ways. The clear commands train their ear. Then, when something less clear comes up, they have a growing sense of God because they have been walking with him in the things that were never in doubt.

<InsightCard label="The paradigm in one line">
A new believer does not obey because they can already hear God. They learn to hear God because they obey. Start with the clear commands, and discernment grows out of a life that is actually following them.
</InsightCard>

Jesus said his sheep would come to know his voice (John 10:27), but that knowing is grown, not instant. And he was blunt that hearing without doing is worthless: "Why do you call me 'Lord, Lord,' and do not do what I say?" (Luke 6:46). The doing is not optional, and it is not just proof of discipleship. It is how the discipleship happens.

## Then Release as They Grow

None of this is about keeping people dependent on you. The goal of high direction is to work yourself out of a job. As a new believer proves faithful in the clear things, you release more, and you ask better questions, and you give them more room to discern. Directive early, releasing steadily, until they can feed themselves and train someone else.

That arc is the heart of [how to train disciples](/how-to-train-disciples/) and the reason the [MAWL pattern](/mawl-model-assist-watch-leave/) exists: you model and are highly directive at first, then assist, then watch, then leave. High direction is the first move of a plan whose whole aim is release. If you are still spoon-feeding someone a year in, you have missed the point as badly as if you had abandoned them on day one.

## How to Actually Do It

The paradigm is simple; the practice is where it lives. When a person first believes, walk them through their first weeks with clear, doable steps: teach them to feed themselves in Scripture and obey what they read, build the basic habits, and keep the rhythm simple. That is exactly what [new believer follow-up](/new-believer-follow-up/) lays out for the first thirty days, what the [discipleship training curriculum](/discipleship-training-curriculum/) provides as a sequence of starter tools, and what a repeatable pattern like [Three Thirds](/three-thirds/) gives a group every week: read, obey, and pass it on. Use those for the how. This article is just the why underneath them.

<WarningCard label="Two ways to get it wrong">
There are two errors, and most of us lean toward one. The first is staying directive forever, keeping people dependent on you, which is control dressed up as discipleship. The second, more common among us who love freedom, is being too hands-off too early, leaving a new believer to "follow the Spirit" before they can tell the Spirit from their own preferences. That is not respect, it is neglect. Be clear and directive at the start, and release deliberately as they grow. Both the holding on and the letting go are acts of love.
</WarningCard>

<PracticeCard label="Start This Week">
1. **Give one clear next step.** With the new believer you are helping, name one plain command of Jesus to obey this week. Do not ask them to discern it. Tell them, kindly.
2. **Do it with them.** Show them how, or do it alongside them, so obeying feels possible, not abstract.
3. **Follow up and add one.** Check in on how the obedience went, celebrate it, and add the next clear step. Let their faithfulness in the small, clear things set the pace.
</PracticeCard>

## Frequently asked questions

### Why train new believers in a directive way?

Because they cannot yet hear God well. Discernment is trained "by constant use" over time (Hebrews 5:14), and a new believer has not done that yet. Leaving them to navigate by their own sense of God's voice usually just returns their own impressions to them. Clear, directive obedience to Jesus' plain commands gives them solid ground to stand on and is the very thing that trains their ear to know him.

### How do new believers learn to hear God?

By obeying what he has already clearly said. Jesus promised that whoever keeps his commands is the one he will show himself to (John 14:21). Obedience to the clear comes first, and deeper knowing follows, so a new believer grows into recognizing God's voice by walking with him in the things that were never in doubt, not by trying to discern the unclear before they are ready.

### How do I disciple a new Christian?

Start clear and directive: give one plain command or step to obey, do it with them, follow up, and add the next as they are faithful. Keep the habits simple and reproducible. For the practical path, use [new believer follow-up](/new-believer-follow-up/) for the first thirty days and the [discipleship training curriculum](/discipleship-training-curriculum/) for the tools, then release more responsibility as they mature.

### Isn't being directive the same as being controlling?

No, as long as you are aiming at release. Directive means clear and kind with a beginner who does not yet know the way; controlling means keeping them dependent on you. Good training is highly directive at the start and steadily hands off responsibility, so the person can soon feed themselves and disciple someone else. The test is whether your direction is making them more able to follow Jesus without you, or less.
