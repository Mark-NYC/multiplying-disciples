That is the whole model in four words, but the four words hide the hard part. Most of us are comfortable doing the work ourselves and even comfortable letting someone help. Handing it over completely, and walking away while they lead, is where training usually breaks down. MAWL is built to get you all the way there.

## What MAWL Stands For

MAWL describes four phases of training the same person to do something you can already do, whether that is sharing the gospel, leading a group, or discipling someone. Across the four phases, one thing steadily changes: who is doing the work. You do less each stage. They do more.

<InsightCard label="Model, Assist, Watch, Leave">
**Model.** You do it while they watch. You share the gospel, lead the meeting, or follow up on last week's obedience, with them beside you seeing how it is actually done.

**Assist.** You do it together. They take a piece of it, and you are right there to help, encourage, and fill the gaps.

**Watch.** They do it while you watch. Now they are leading, and you have become the helper, giving honest feedback afterward.

**Leave.** They do it without you, and they start training someone else the same way.
</InsightCard>

Notice the handoff. In Model you are carrying all of it. By Watch you have gone quiet, and they are carrying almost all of it. Leave is not you disappearing on people. It is you trusting them with the work and turning your attention to the next person, while they now turn theirs to someone new.

## Train With the End in Mind

MAWL is used widely in church-planting and disciple-making training. The point most people miss is simple: you train with the end in mind from the very first day. The goal is not to create someone who depends on you. It is to create someone who can do the work without you and pass it on. If you never plan to leave, you are not really training, you are just doing ministry with an audience.

That changes how you model. You model briefly and on purpose, not for months, and you move a person to the next phase as fast as they can handle it. Pair high challenge with high grace: expect a lot, hand off real responsibility early, and stay close enough to catch people when they stumble. The aim is release, and release should come sooner than feels comfortable.

If you want to dig deeper into the model, the church planter Curtis Sergeant is a helpful resource on it.

## The Phase Almost Everyone Skips: Leave

Here is the pattern you will see again and again. A trainer is happy to Model. They will even Assist, because helping still keeps them in charge. Then they get stuck. They keep demonstrating, keep leading, keep being needed, and they never actually let the other person take it and run.

The reasons feel responsible. They are not ready yet. They might do it wrong. It is faster if I just do it. All of that can be true, and it still stalls everything, because a disciple who is never released never becomes a maker of disciples. You cannot steer a parked car. At some point you have to let them drive, knowing they will grind the gears a little.

This is why the last two phases matter more than the first two. Watch is where you bite your tongue and let them lead a meeting you could have led better. Leave is where you accept that their version, done by them and reproduced in others, beats your version done perfectly and stopping with you. Letting go well is its own skill, and it is worth learning on purpose: [how to step back without abandoning new leaders](/how-to-step-back-without-abandoning-new-leaders/) is the deep version of the "Leave" in MAWL.

<WarningCard label="Leaving is not abandoning">
Leave does not mean vanish. It means the responsibility moves to them while the relationship stays. Jesus left his disciples the mission and the Spirit, not a cold shoulder. Done badly, "leaving" drops people before they can stand. Done well, it releases them while you stay reachable, cheer them on, and keep an eye out from a step back. The move is from control to trust, not from present to gone.
</WarningCard>

## MAWL and How Jesus Trained

MAWL is not a command in the Bible, and no verse spells out four letters. It is a way of describing how Jesus actually trained the Twelve. He appointed them to be with him and then to be sent out:

<ScriptureBlock reference="Mark 3:14">
He appointed twelve that they might be with him and that he might send them out to preach.
</ScriptureBlock>

They watched him teach, heal, and reach people. He sent them out in pairs to do the same and come back to talk it through. Then he left, handing them the mission and the Holy Spirit, and the book of Acts is what "Leave" looked like when it worked. If you want that biblical pattern laid out in full, it is the backbone of [how to train disciples](/how-to-train-disciples/), the training pillar this article sits under. MAWL is simply the memorable shorthand for it.

Because it mirrors how people actually learn, MAWL works for almost anything reproducible: leading a [simple church](/what-is-a-church-planting-movement/) gathering, running a Bible study, doing follow-up, or [raising up new leaders](/how-to-identify-and-train-emerging-leaders/). It is one of the ordinary patterns that, reproduced over and over, is how [disciples and churches multiply](/how-to-multiply-disciples-and-churches/).

## How to Start Using MAWL This Week

You do not need a curriculum to begin. You need one person and one skill.

<PracticeCard label="Try It With One Person">
1. **Pick one thing and one person.** Choose a single reproducible skill you already do, like sharing your testimony or leading a short Bible discussion, and one person you are discipling.
2. **Model it once, on purpose.** Do it with them beside you, and tell them plainly: next time, part of this is yours.
3. **Hand off a piece (Assist), then hand off the lead (Watch).** Give them a real part now, and the whole thing soon, while you stay close to encourage and give feedback.
4. **Name the finish line out loud.** Tell them from the start that the goal is for them to do this without you and teach the next person. Then, when they are ready, actually leave.
</PracticeCard>

The test of MAWL is not whether you can recite it. It is whether, a few months from now, someone you trained is training someone you have never met.

## Frequently asked questions

### What does MAWL stand for?

MAWL stands for Model, Assist, Watch, Leave. It is a four-phase way to train someone: you do the task while they watch (Model), you do it together (Assist), they do it while you watch and coach (Watch), and then you release them to do it on their own and train the next person (Leave). The responsibility shifts from you to them across the four phases.

### Why do most trainers never reach Leave?

Because the first two phases keep them in charge, and letting go feels risky. It is faster and safer to keep doing the work yourself or to keep leading while someone assists. But a disciple who is never released never becomes someone who makes disciples, so getting to Leave is the whole point. The key is to train with that release in mind from the start and to hand off responsibility sooner than feels comfortable.

### Is MAWL in the Bible?

Not by name. MAWL is a modern way of describing how Jesus trained his disciples: he let them be with him and watch him work, sent them out to do the same, and then left them the mission and the Holy Spirit (Mark 3:14; Acts). It is a helpful method, not a biblical command, and it should serve obedience to Jesus rather than replace it.

### How is MAWL different from just discipling someone?

Discipling is the relationship; MAWL is a pattern for the training inside it. Plenty of discipling never moves past teaching and demonstrating. MAWL adds the deliberate handoff, making sure the person you disciple actually takes the work over and passes it on, so it keeps reproducing after you step back.
