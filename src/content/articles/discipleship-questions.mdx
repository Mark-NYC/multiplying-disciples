That is the difference between a discipleship relationship that grows someone and one that just meets. The questions you ask set the ceiling. Ask vague questions and you get vague disciples. Ask questions that go somewhere, and people actually move.

## Why Most Discipleship Questions Go Nowhere

Think about the questions we usually reach for. "How are you doing spiritually?" "How is your walk with God?" "How is your heart?" They sound caring, and they almost always produce the same thing: a fuzzy, self-graded answer. "Pretty good." "A little dry lately." Then you nod, and the conversation moves on, and nothing changes.

The trouble is not that these questions are wrong. It is that they measure a feeling instead of a life. They ask a person to rate their spirituality, which invites either performance or vague guilt, and neither one leads anywhere. You can answer "my walk is good" while obeying nothing Jesus said this week, and you can answer "I'm struggling" and never be asked to take a single step.

<PullQuote>Ask vague questions and you get vague disciples. The questions you ask set the ceiling.</PullQuote>

## Questions That Go Somewhere Are About Obedience

The fix is to move your questions off of feelings and onto obedience. Jesus never measured his followers by how spiritual they felt. He measured them by whether they did what he said:

<ScriptureBlock reference="Luke 6:46">
Why do you call me, "Lord, Lord," and do not do what I say?
</ScriptureBlock>

James says the same thing in one line: be doers of the word, and not hearers only, who deceive themselves (James 1:22). So the most useful discipleship question is almost always some version of one thing: what did God show you, and did you do it? That single shift, from "how do you feel about God" to "what did God say and did you obey it," changes everything, because now the conversation is about a real life, and it points at a real next step.

Almost every discipleship question that goes somewhere is a form of one question: what did God show you, and did you obey it? Start there, and honesty, growth, and a next step tend to follow on their own.
</InsightCard>

## A Simple Set of Questions

You do not need a long list. You need a few good questions you ask every time, so they become a rhythm rather than an event. Here is a simple set, grouped by direction. It mirrors the look-back, look-up, look-forward pattern of a [Three Thirds](/three-thirds/) gathering, and it works one-on-one or in a group.

**Look back (obedience).** This is the anchor. Start here every time.

- What did God show you in his Word since we last met?
- Did you do the thing you said you would do last time?
- Where did you obey, and where did you get stuck?

**Look in (honesty).** Now get past the report to the person.

- How are you really doing, under the surface?
- What are you wrestling with that you have not said out loud?
- Where do you need help right now?

**Look out (mission).** A disciple is always also a fisher, so ask about the people they are reaching.

- Who are you praying for who does not yet know Jesus?
- Who did you have a spiritual conversation with, or share your story with?
- Who are you helping follow Jesus, and how is that going?

**Look forward (next step).** Never close without this.

- Given all that, what is your one next step of obedience this week?
- Who will you tell, so someone checks in with you on it?

That is the whole toolkit. The same questions every week, asked honestly, will disciple a person further than any curriculum, because they keep pointing back to obeying Jesus and forward to the next real step. If you want the fuller philosophy under this, it is [obedience-based discipleship](/obedience-based-discipleship/), and the habits these questions build are [the 3 core habits of a disciple](/the-3-core-habits-of-a-disciple/).

## Always End With a Next Step

Here is the rule that separates a question that goes somewhere from one that does not: it ends in a commitment. If your time together does not produce one clear, spoken next step of obedience, the conversation went nowhere, however good it felt.

So make the last question count. Have the person name the step themselves, out loud, in their own words. Write it down. Then, next time, the very first question is about that step. That loop, obey, report back, obey again, is what [friendly accountability](/unlock-the-power-of-friendly-accountability/) actually is, and it is how ordinary conversations start producing real change. Scripture calls us to it: encourage one another daily, so no one is hardened by sin's deceit (Hebrews 3:13).

## The Point Is Not the Questions

Do not miss what these questions are for. They are simple enough that the person you disciple can turn around and ask them of someone else next month. That is the goal. You are not just growing a disciple. You are handing them a way of discipling that reproduces, so the conversation you are having can happen one relationship over, without you in the room. That is the whole move of [how to make disciples](/how-to-make-disciples/).

<WarningCard label="Ask like a brother, not an interrogator">
Questions about obedience can turn into a courtroom fast, and that kills the very honesty you are after. Guard against it. Ask from love, not to catch someone out, and restore gently when they have failed rather than piling on (Galatians 6:1-2). The fastest way to keep it safe is to answer every question yourself first: tell them what God showed you and where you obeyed or did not, before you ask them. You are a fellow disciple who needs the same grace, not a boss doing a review.
</WarningCard>

<PracticeCard label="Try It This Week">
1. **Pick your handful of questions.** Choose one from each direction: look back, look in, look out, look forward. Keep it short.
2. **Answer them yourself first.** In your next discipleship conversation, share your own answers before you ask, especially the obedience one.
3. **Close with a named step.** Have the other person say one next step of obedience out loud, write it down, and agree to start there next time.
</PracticeCard>

## Frequently asked questions

### What questions should I ask when discipling someone?

Ask a few good questions every time, grouped by direction: look back ("What did God show you in his Word, and did you do what you said you would?"), look in ("How are you really doing, and where do you need help?"), look out ("Who are you praying for and sharing with?"), and look forward ("What is your one next step of obedience this week?"). The same simple set, asked honestly each time, disciples a person further than any curriculum.

### Why do most discipleship conversations go nowhere?

Because they run on vague, feelings-based questions like "How is your walk with God?" that invite a fuzzy, self-graded answer and never lead to action. They measure how spiritual someone feels instead of whether they are actually obeying Jesus, and they rarely end in a concrete next step. Questions tied to obedience, that finish with a named commitment, are what produce real movement.

### What are good accountability questions?

Good accountability questions are specific and about obedience, not feelings: "Did you do the thing you said you would do last time?" "Where did you obey, and where did you get stuck?" "Who are you sharing Jesus with?" and "What is your next step, and who will check in with you on it?" Ask them from love, answer them yourself first, and always restore gently rather than condemn (Galatians 6:1).

### How is this different from just having deep conversations?

Deep conversations are good, but they can circle forever without changing anything. Discipleship questions that go somewhere are aimed: they keep returning to what God said and whether it was obeyed, and they always end with a next step someone commits to out loud. The point is not a better talk. It is a life that keeps taking real steps of obedience and helping someone else do the same.
