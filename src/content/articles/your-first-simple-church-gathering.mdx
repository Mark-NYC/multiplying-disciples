The night before your first simple church gathering, the temptation is to over-prepare: a worship playlist, a teaching outline, a plan for every minute. Resist it. The first church did not gather around a production. It gathered around Jesus, in homes, over food, in the Word and prayer. Your first meeting should feel less like a service you are hosting and more like a family sitting down together. Here is exactly how to do it.

## Before you meet: who and what

Two decisions matter before the first gathering: who is coming, and what you will need. Keep both simple.

<InsightCard label="Before your first gathering">
**Who to invite.** Start with the people already close to you: the believers and new believers in your oikos, family, and friendships. You are gathering a spiritual family, so invite the people you are already walking with, not strangers to fill a room. A handful is plenty.

**Where and when.** A home, around a table, at a regular time people can actually keep. Consistency matters more than the setting.

**What to bring.** Something to eat, enough Bibles for everyone (or a phone app), and one short passage picked ahead of time. That is the whole supply list.

**Who leads.** You facilitate the first one, but plan from the start to hand pieces to others quickly. The goal is a family where anyone can lead, not a meeting that depends on you.
</InsightCard>

Notice there is no mention of a sound system, a curriculum, or a budget. A simple church gathering is meant to be reproducible, which means everything about it should be simple enough for someone in the room to copy next month.

## A simple format for your first gathering

The most reproducible way to run the meeting is the [Three Thirds](/three-thirds/) format: you spend roughly a third of your time looking back, a third looking up into God's Word, and a third looking ahead to obedience. Here is how that plays out the first time you gather.

<InsightCard label="A first gathering, in three parts">
**1. Look back (welcome, worship, care).** Eat together. Let people share how they are actually doing. Thank God and worship simply, even just a song or a few prayers of praise. If anyone is already following Jesus, ask how their week with him went. You are building a family, so start with real life.

**2. Look up (open the Word).** Read the short passage you chose, together. Then ask a few simple questions anyone can answer: What does this say about God? What does it say about people? What is one thing Jesus is asking us to do? Keep the talking spread around the room. The leader is a facilitator, not a lecturer.

**3. Look ahead (obey and pray).** Before you close, have each person name one thing they will do this week in response to the passage, and one person they could share it with. Pray for each other by name. Set the next time to meet. Then send everyone out with something to actually do.
</InsightCard>

That is a full simple church gathering. It has worship, the Word, real fellowship, prayer, and a step of obedience, and none of it required a professional. Jesus said this is enough for him to be present.

<ScriptureBlock reference="Matthew 18:20">
For where two or three gather in my name, there am I with them.
</ScriptureBlock>

## Keep the focus on obeying, not performing

The single most important thing you can do in a first gathering is protect the doing. It is easy to let the meeting drift into a good discussion that ends with everyone a little more informed and nothing changed. The Three Thirds format guards against that by ending every gathering with obedience, and Scripture is blunt about why that matters.

<ScriptureBlock reference="James 1:22">
Do not merely listen to the word, and so deceive yourselves. Do what it says.
</ScriptureBlock>

So do not measure your first gathering by how smoothly it ran or how good your questions were. Measure it by whether people left with something to obey and someone to tell. That is the habit that turns a meeting into a church.

## Keep it simple enough to repeat

A first gathering usually runs about an hour to ninety minutes, but the clock is not the point. Reproducibility is. After the meeting, ask yourself one question: could someone who was in the room tonight lead this next time? If the answer is yes, you have done it right. If the meeting depended on your personality, your knowledge, or your preparation, simplify until it does not.

That is how a first gathering becomes a second, and a single simple church eventually becomes two. You are not building an audience that comes to watch you. You are starting a family that can reproduce.

<PracticeCard>
Set a date for your first gathering in the next two weeks. Write down who you will invite, pick one short passage (a story from the Gospel of Mark works well), and plan the three parts: look back, look up, look ahead. Keep it simple enough that you could hand the whole thing to someone else at the second meeting.
</PracticeCard>

## Frequently asked questions

### What do you do at a first simple church gathering?

Gather a few people around a meal and an open Bible, and move through three simple parts. Look back by sharing real life, worshiping simply, and celebrating how people followed Jesus this week. Look up by reading one short passage and asking what it says about God, about people, and what Jesus is asking you to do. Look ahead by having each person name one act of obedience and one person to share it with, then pray for each other and set the next meeting. Keep it simple enough that someone else could lead it next time.

### Who do I invite to a simple church?

Start with the people already close to you: believers and new believers in your oikos, your family, and your friendships. A simple church is a spiritual family, so you invite the people you are already walking with rather than trying to fill a room with strangers. A handful of people is plenty to begin, since Jesus said he is present wherever two or three gather in his name (Matthew 18:20).

### How long should a simple church meeting be?

Usually about an hour to ninety minutes, but the length matters less than the shape. Aim to spend roughly a third of your time looking back (welcome, worship, care), a third looking up into the Word, and a third looking ahead to obedience and prayer. What matters most is that people leave with something to obey and someone to tell, and that the meeting stays simple enough to reproduce.
