# Multiplying Disciples Editorial Voice

This file governs the voice of every article, landing page, resource, email, and substantial piece of prose in the Multiplying Disciples repository.

It does not replace factual research, theological review, the article system, or the repository playbook. It controls how the final writing sounds.

## The standard

Write like Mark Goering talking to a serious practitioner over coffee.

Mark is a disciple maker and church planter working in New York City. He writes from the field, not from a stage or a content desk. His voice is direct, biblical, practical, and hopeful. He respects the reader enough to speak plainly.

The writing should sound like someone who has tried the tools, trained real people, made mistakes, and kept going.

It should never sound like:

- a marketing department
- a seminary paper
- a generic devotional
- an AI summary
- a movement expert trying to impress other movement experts
- a scolding pastor talking down to passive Christians

## The reader

Write primarily for ordinary Christians and ministry leaders who want to make disciples but may not know what to do next.

Assume the reader:

- loves Jesus
- is busy
- may feel underprepared
- has heard more teaching than they have practiced
- needs a clear next step, not another pile of information
- can handle a direct challenge when it comes with a real path forward

Do not write as though the reader already knows movement jargon. Explain a term the first time it appears, or replace it with normal language.

## Source order

When sources differ in voice, use this order:

1. Mark's current draft, transcript, field note, or direct instruction
2. Mark's published Substack writing
3. Mark's approved Multiplying Disciples and CoVo Multiplier articles
4. This file
5. SEO conventions

SEO never outranks voice, biblical accuracy, or clarity.

Preserve Mark's actual stories, claims, convictions, and useful rough edges. Clean up confusion. Do not polish the life out of the writing.

## Core voice

### Plainspoken

Use common words. Say the thing directly.

Prefer:

- use
- help
- start
- obey
- practice
- show
- train
- send
- try
- change

Avoid inflated substitutes such as:

- utilize
- facilitate
- activate
- optimize
- cultivate
- operationalize
- catalyze
- implement, when "do" works

Movement terms such as "catalyst" may be used when they name a real role. Do not use them to make ordinary work sound more important.

### Concrete

Move quickly from an idea to a person, place, action, decision, or example.

Weak:

> Disciplemaking flourishes through intentional relational environments.

Better:

> Open the Bible with someone. Help them obey what Jesus says. Then show them how to do the same with someone else.

Use real details when they are supplied. Never invent a person, conversation, location, result, statistic, or ministry story.

If a needed detail is missing, leave a clear note for the author instead of filling the gap with a plausible story.

### Field-tested

Write from practice. Name what works, what breaks down, and what the reader can try this week.

Do not pretend every tool works in every setting. Do not oversell multiplication. Make room for slow progress, failure, adjustment, suffering, and the work of the Holy Spirit.

### Biblical

Let Scripture carry weight without turning the article into a string of proof texts.

- Read verses in context.
- Distinguish a biblical command from a useful method.
- Distinguish what a passage describes from what it requires.
- Do not claim the Bible teaches a modern framework by name.
- Do not imply that one tool is the faithful way to obey Jesus.
- Do not flatten disciplemaking, evangelism, leadership, and church into one undefined activity.
- Keep grace, the gospel, obedience, the Holy Spirit, and the local church in view where the subject calls for them.

When the biblical basis is uncertain, flag it. Do not smooth over the problem with confident language.

### Challenging without shaming

Challenge passivity, overtraining, fear, and ministry habits that do not produce obedience. Give the reader a doable response.

Do not caricature churches, pastors, programs, or Christians who use a different method. Critique the limitation precisely.

Weak:

> Churches have failed because they only care about attendance.

Better:

> Gathering matters. But attendance alone does not teach someone how to make a disciple.

### Hopeful, not hyped

Use confidence without promising results that belong to God.

Do not call every idea:

- powerful
- profound
- transformative
- revolutionary
- game-changing
- essential
- critical

If the point matters, show why it matters.

## Rhythm and sentence shape

Vary sentence length naturally. Some sentences can be short. Others should carry a complete thought.

Use contractions when Mark would use them in conversation.

Keep paragraphs fairly short, usually one to four sentences. Do not force every sentence into its own paragraph.

Prefer active voice.

Use questions when they create honest reflection. Do not stack rhetorical questions to manufacture energy.

Never use em dashes.

Use colons, commas, parentheses, or a new sentence instead.

Semicolons are allowed but should be rare.

Sentence fragments are allowed when they sound spoken and earn their place. Do not build whole sections out of fragments.

## Patterns to remove

Delete or rewrite these common AI habits:

### Canned openings

- "In today's world..."
- "In a culture where..."
- "In the landscape of..."
- "Throughout history..."
- "Imagine a world where..."
- "What if I told you..."
- a dictionary definition used only to start the article
- a broad problem statement with no person, tension, or scene

### Fake depth

- "This truth invites us to..."
- "There is something profound about..."
- "This serves as a powerful reminder..."
- "At its core..."
- "The beauty of this approach is..."
- "It is important to note..."
- "The invitation is simple..."
- "This is where the magic happens."
- "It changes everything."

### Overused vocabulary

Avoid these unless the exact word is necessary:

- quietly
- delve
- intricate
- tapestry
- foster
- leverage
- unlock
- robust
- landscape
- navigate
- journey
- realm
- transformative
- holistic
- dynamic
- impactful
- resonate

### Manufactured contrast

Do not rely on formulas such as:

- "It's not about X. It's about Y."
- "This isn't just X. It's Y."
- "Not X. Not Y. Just Z."
- "We don't need more X. We need Y."
- "The goal isn't X; the goal is Y."

A contrast can be true and useful. State it in a fresh, specific way and do not build the article out of repeated oppositions.

Weak:

> Disciplemaking is not a program. It is a lifestyle.

Better:

> A program can create space for disciplemaking. The real work happens as people follow Jesus together in ordinary life.

### Artificial punchiness

Avoid strings like:

> No stage. No program. No permission needed.

Avoid ending several paragraphs in a row with a dramatic three-word sentence.

Avoid repeated one-line paragraphs designed to sound quotable.

Do not force every section into a three-part list.

### Empty transitions

Cut phrases such as:

- "Let's dive in."
- "Let's explore."
- "Now, let's turn to..."
- "With that in mind..."
- "As we have seen..."
- "In conclusion..."
- "The bottom line is..."

Move to the next point.

## Openings

Open with one of these:

- a real tension the reader feels
- a supplied field story
- a concrete observation
- a clear claim with stakes
- a biblical scene handled in context

Get to the article's actual point within the first few paragraphs.

Do not spend 300 words warming up. Do not create a fictional scene. Do not tease a secret that the article never delivers.

## Headings and structure

Use headings that tell the reader what the section is about.

Prefer natural phrases over clever labels. A heading can create curiosity, but it should not hide the point.

Do not force every article into the same numbered structure. Let the material decide the shape.

Each section should do at least one job:

- explain
- show
- correct
- answer an objection
- apply
- move the argument forward

If a section repeats the previous section in new words, cut it.

Lists should contain real parallel items. Do not convert normal prose into bullets simply to break up the page.

## Stories, examples, and ministry claims

Only use a story when its source is clear.

Never:

- merge separate real people into a composite without Mark's approval
- add dialogue Mark did not provide
- create a conversion, healing, church, or multiplication result
- attach a location to a story without confirmation
- make an uncertain result sound verified
- treat an AI-generated or stock image as a real field photograph

When anonymity matters, remove identifying details without changing the meaning of the story.

If a result is early, say it is early. If it is a hope, call it a hope. If it did not multiply, do not imply that it did.

## Methods and tools

Multiplying Disciples uses practical tools, but the tools serve obedience. They are not the point.

When introducing a tool:

1. Name the problem it helps solve.
2. Explain it in normal language.
3. Show how someone would use it.
4. Name its limits when relevant.
5. Connect it to a clear next step.

Do not stack tool names to signal expertise.

Do not imply that using the 3 Circles, Three Thirds, Four Fields, Church Circle, DBS, MAWL, Oikos, or another framework guarantees multiplication.

Do not standardize a personal practice into a universal biblical rule. This matters especially with prayer rhythms, meeting formats, timelines, and numerical goals.

## Calls to action

End with a next step that fits the article.

Good next steps are specific:

- write the names of five people you already know
- share your 15-second testimony with one person this week
- invite someone to read one Gospel story with you
- practice the tool with a friend before you use it

Do not end with a vague command to "live this out" or "embrace the journey."

Use one primary site call to action. Do not pile on unrelated offers.

## SEO without losing the voice

Use the primary phrase naturally in the title, opening, one useful heading, and body when it fits.

Do not:

- repeat an exact keyword until it sounds unnatural
- add a section only because a competing article has one
- answer the same question several ways to increase word count
- write headings that no human would say
- bury Mark's strongest claim under search-engine throat-clearing

Answer the searcher's real question early. Then give them something more useful than the current results.

## Recurring ideas

These ideas belong to Mark's body of work. Use them only when they fit the subject:

- obedience needs practice, not information alone
- ordinary disciples can make disciples
- people discover gifts while serving
- you cannot steer a parked car
- train people in a church and help them start a church
- move from lost person to disciple to leader
- give people enough to obey and pass on
- multiplication requires release, not control
- shared life matters alongside teaching, prayer, and gathering

Do not sprinkle these into every article as slogans. Do not turn them into branded catchphrases unless the page is specifically teaching the concept.

## Editing Mark's draft

Protect the substance.

Do not replace Mark's specific wording with a smoother generic version simply because it sounds more polished.

When editing:

1. Preserve his claim, story, and theological intent.
2. Remove repetition that does not add force.
3. Clarify sentences that make the reader work too hard.
4. Replace abstractions with supplied concrete details.
5. Check Scripture and factual claims.
6. Remove AI patterns.
7. Read the result aloud.

If a sentence sounds like something Mark would never say, rewrite it.

If a rough sentence carries personality, improve its clarity without sterilizing it.

Do not make large structural or theological changes silently. Explain them in the work summary.

## Drafting from a transcript

A transcript is source material, not finished prose.

Keep:

- the central claim
- real stories and details
- distinctive phrases
- honest tension
- useful repetition that carries emphasis

Remove:

- verbal filler
- circular explanation
- repeated setup
- false starts
- side trails that do not serve the article

Do not turn spoken material into formal essay language. Keep the sense that a real person is speaking to the reader.

## Before publishing

Run this check on the final draft:

- Does the opening reach the real point quickly?
- Would Mark say these sentences out loud?
- Is the writing concrete?
- Are all stories and results sourced?
- Are biblical claims accurate and in context?
- Have methods remained methods rather than commands?
- Does the article challenge without shaming?
- Does each section add something?
- Are the headings natural?
- Is the keyword use invisible?
- Is there one clear next step?
- Are there any em dashes?
- Are there canned AI phrases or repeated contrast formulas?
- Can the draft lose 10 percent without losing meaning?

If the answer to the last question is yes, cut it.

## Short voice test

The finished piece should feel:

- more like a field note than a white paper
- more like coaching than lecturing
- more like conviction than hype
- more like a real conversation than content production
- more interested in helping the reader obey Jesus than proving the writer knows the subject

Clear beats clever. Specific beats impressive. Honest beats polished.
